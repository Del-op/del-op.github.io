I think of a theoretical framework as a construction that tries to mimic reality, but at some point, you notice deviations from reality, which makes this construction a unique world on its own. The chance of constructing a world that exactly represents the real one is almost zero, but the chance that you would make a construction that shares properties with reality is very high (with good reasoning). I think that these shared properties across many frameworks are what would enable us to have a broader and more powerful framework that represents the universe better.

It is likely that the universe is a total mess of complexity, a complexity in the sense of details, but one that you can reason about and find fascinating.

Frameworks should be seen for what they are, attempts to model reality, not reality itself. Analysis can be directed toward a framework’s properties that align with reality, investigating their association with reality. It could happen that a property is partially true, or that a property’s outcomes coincide with reality within some finite range but deviate beyond it.

This connects with **epistemology**, which naturally raises the question of the **limits of knowledge**: can a framework ever fully “capture” reality, or will it always remain partial, bound by the assumptions, simplifications, and boundaries we impose? Perhaps every framework, no matter how refined, is destined to be incomplete, offering only a perspective rather than a total picture.

At the same time, the strength of a framework does not lie only in its perfect alignment with reality but also in its **falsifiability**, its capacity to be tested, to fail, and to evolve. A framework gains credibility not because it mirrors reality flawlessly, but because it opens itself to be tested, and through that process, it can be corrected, refined, or even replaced.

This also points toward the value of **pluralism**. Multiple frameworks can coexist, each capturing different useful aspects of reality, sometimes overlapping, sometimes contradicting, yet all contributing to a more layered understanding. Reality may be too vast for any single construction, but through the interaction of many frameworks, each illuminating a different angle, we would probably approach a more meaningful approximation of the universe.

Perhaps the beauty lies not in achieving a perfect framework, but in the ongoing refinement and overlap between many frameworks.
